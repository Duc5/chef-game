2. Three main ingredients I'd implement


1. Dewcrown Mushroom
Riverwood Forest · Environmental manipulation

A legendary mushroom that only grows on an island surrounded by flowing freshwater.

NPC clue: An old lumberjack remember the mushroom is in an island.

Puzzle: The player finds a fallen axe, a pile of stones, and a tall tree on the riverbank. The stones can be positioned to stop the tree from falling in the wrong direction. Three whacks with the spatula topple it, forming a bridge.

Discovery: The player learns that attacking environmental objects can permanently change the world.


2. Skyfeather Egg
Whispering Peaks · Experimentation and movement

A rare ingredient found in the nest of a magical bird that lives on an otherwise inaccessible cliff.

NPC clue: A retired mountaineer remember the wind is very strong in that velley.

Puzzle: The player throws rocks into apparently empty sections of a chasm. Some fall, but others float upwards, revealing invisible columns of rising air. The player must jump between the safe wind currents to reach the nest.

Discovery: Objects can reveal hidden environmental mechanics. The solution isn't visible until the player experiments.


3. Cinderheart Pepper
Emberfall Volcano · Combat and puzzle integration

A fiery pepper that grows inside a sealed volcanic greenhouse, fed by the heat of the mountain.

NPC clue: A travelling blacksmith explains that the ancient forge was powered by returning fire to its source.

Puzzle: Fire spirits shoot projectiles towards the chef. By timing the spatula swing, the player can reflect the projectiles. Reflected fireballs can hurt the spirits or activate three ancient furnace switches to open the greenhouse.

Discovery: Combat mechanics double as puzzle-solving tools.

4. Frostglass Salt — The Frozen Mirror Lake

The lake is full of reflective ice. A reclusive fisherman says that moonlight once opened a hidden passage. The player slides ice blocks into position to redirect beams of light onto a frozen cave door. Some blocks slide until they hit obstacles, so placement matters.

Interactions and white outlines
Here's what should happen whenever the player approaches something interactive:
1. A white pixel outline appears on the nearest eligible object within range.
2. An interaction prompt appears, such as E: Talk or E: Pick up.
3. Hittable objects additionally respond to the spatula, without needing an E interaction.
4. When multiple objects are nearby, the game chooses the nearest valid object, favouring the character's facing direction.
5. Objects outside interaction range lose their outline.
Use a reusable interaction component and outline shader. Don't write separate proximity-handling code for every NPC, rock, tree and ingredient.

Optional hint system: 3 hints per puzzle progressively more disclosed
The player should always be able to solve a puzzle without reading the hints. Environmental details should provide enough information through observation and experimentation. Hints are an accessibility feature, not the primary way to communicate a puzzle solution.

Quest progression
speaking to NPC is not compulsory
NPCs provide clues, but don't gate the actual ingredient. For example, a player who discovers the wind puzzle without speaking to the mountaineer can still complete it.

The narrative path would be:
Pigeon delivers the royal letter

Player examines ingredient list

Player explores and optionally questions NPCs

Player discovers and solves puzzle

Ingredient is collected and journal updated

All three collected → return to Royal Kitchen

Minimpa behavior
Areas within roughly six map tiles of the player become discovered, with walls blocking reveal if desired.

Previously visited tiles remain visible permanently. Undiscovered tiles remain dark. NPCs, points of interest and region entrances only appear after discovery.

A small minimap stays in the top-right corner. Pressing M opens a larger region map. Discovery is preserved when the player moves between regions or reloads a save.



For the prototype, use a grid-based discovery mask rather than expensive line-of-sight simulation or continuous texture painting. This is easier to test and maintain.

Recommended Godot project architecture
For code readability, editability and inspection, I recommend a scene-component architecture with data-driven quests.

Godot's own best-practices documentation recommends self-contained scenes, low coupling and clearly owned responsibilities. Its Resource system is also suitable for defining reusable gameplay data independently of code.

Here's an example of the kind of separation I want Codex to follow.

When the chef cuts down the tree:
1. The attack hitbox detects a collision with the tree's damageable component.
2. The tree's local puzzle controller counts the hits.
3. After three hits, the tree changes state to FALLEN.
4. Its animation plays, and the bridge collision becomes traversable.
5. The tree emits an event such as puzzle_completed("forest_tree_bridge").
6. The quest manager updates relevant progression, without the tree needing to know which quest owns it.
This is much more maintainable than a giant game_manager.gd script controlling every puzzle.
For the environment, use Godot's TileMapLayer nodes for terrain, obstacles and foreground elements. The older TileMap node is deprecated in Godot 4.

What Codex should document
Each major system needs a short explanation of its purpose, public signals, dependencies and extension points.

For example, adding a new ingredient quest should generally involve creating a quest-data resource, placing ingredient and puzzle scenes, and writing the new puzzle-specific logic. It should not require editing the player controller, inventory UI or every existing quest.

That is the architectural outcome worth testing.

Development phases
1. Phase 1 — Technical foundation
   
   Set up the Godot project, input system, player controller, camera, collision layers, basic UI and reusable interactions. Build a tiny test room. Nothing else until movement and attacking feel good.
2. Phase 2 — One complete gameplay slice
   
   Build the village, pigeon letter, one NPC, a dialogue system, the tree bridge puzzle, the first ingredient and the quest journal. Establish the complete core loop.
3. Phase 3 — Reusable combat
   
   Add spatula hitboxes, dodge, health, projectiles, reflection and the Ash Imp. Test these independently before introducing volcano puzzles.
4. Phase 4 — Remaining quests
   
   Implement wind-jump mechanics and the Skyfeather Egg, then the volcanic greenhouse and Cinderheart Pepper.
5. Phase 5 — Exploration and persistence
   
   Implement minimap discovery, region transitions, saving/loading, checkpoints and puzzle-state persistence.
6. Phase 6 — Art, audio, polish and final testing
   
   Replace placeholders, improve effects and animations, balance combat, verify all quest paths and create a playable desktop export.

Detailed gameplay
The player arrives on a forest path, encounters one Barkling (a small hostile animated stump), and learns that the spatula can damage enemies.

Continuing north reveals an abandoned lumber camp. Bram, an old lumberjack, explains the legend of the mushroom.

Past the camp is a broad river. The player cannot swim across it. A tall weakened tree stands near the riverbank, aligned with the island.

When the player approaches:
- The tree receives the familiar white outline.
- Pressing E inspects the tree, revealing a contextual observation.
- Hitting the trunk three times produces increasingly visible cracks.
- On the third hit, the tree falls across the river and becomes a walkable bridge.
- The player crosses, reaches the glowing mushroom and collects it.
The rock pile is decorative or examinable for this prototype; it is not required for the bridge. We don't need a second tree-direction puzzle.

Seeded variation: Choose between a few handcrafted locations for the river crossing, with the correct tree always visually distinctive and properly aligned. The whole map doesn't need to regenerate.

Checkpoint: Immediately before the crossing clearing.

Completion: The journal updates to show the Dewcrown Mushroom collected. The fallen tree stays fallen when the player returns.

Detailed gameplay
As the player ascends, they notice flags and hanging wind chimes responding to unusually strong wind.

At the chasm, there is no conventional bridge.

A pile of loose rocks sits near the edge. The player can pick one up with E and throw it with right click.

The first thrown rock may fall harmlessly into the chasm. Another, thrown into a hidden updraft, rises into the air.

That is the moment of discovery: there is an invisible path made of wind.

The player must then:
1. Identify the first updraft by throwing rocks.
2. Jump from the ledge and enter the updraft while airborne.
3. Steer towards the next current.
4. Continue through the sequence of currents.
5. Land on the far plateau and collect the Skyfeather Egg.
The updrafts should become faintly visible for a few seconds after a rock enters them, using swirling leaves, snowflakes or wisps of air. Their visibility is temporary, but their locations remain unchanged during the playthrough.

The rock pile must replenish so there is no finite-ammunition softlock.

Seeded variation: Three valid patterns of updraft positions. Each should be individually designed and verified as traversable.

Checkpoint: The jumping ledge. Falling resets the player here without losing collected ingredients.

Important implementation detail: Since this is a top-down game, the map should display a darkened chasm, the player's shadow and a raised sprite during flight. That provides readable feedback about the otherwise hidden height dimension.

Detailed gameplay
The volcano begins with a short passage that introduces a ranged enemy, the Ash Imp.

The imp periodically shoots slow fireballs. The player can dodge them, or time a spatula attack to reflect a fireball back at the enemy.

Further inside, the chef enters the ancient forge.

There are three furnace switches around a sealed door. Each switch must be struck by a reflected projectile.

The player can reflect fireballs from either the Ash Imp or an ancient fire-spouting mechanism positioned in the room. The spout is permanently available so killing the enemy never makes the puzzle impossible.

When all switches are activated:
- The furnace produces a loud metallic sound.
- The sealed door gradually opens.
- A warm green glow shines from the greenhouse.
- The chef can collect the Cinderheart Pepper.
Seeded variation: The furnace switches have three possible handcrafted position arrangements. Their activation order is unrestricted, and every arrangement is reachable using the reflection mechanic.

Checkpoint: The entrance to the Forge Hall.

The reflected projectiles should use the same physics, damage and collision rules whether aimed at enemies or environmental switches. Avoid writing a completely separate puzzle-only projectile system.

Part II — Story, dialogue and puzzle hints
Story tone and characters
I recommend a whimsical British-fantasy tone: charming countryside humour, slightly eccentric villagers, and sincere moments of wonder when the chef discovers something extraordinary.

The chef should be mostly silent, communicating through reactions, short thoughts and occasional dialogue choices that don't alter quest progression. This keeps the script manageable and allows the player to imagine themselves as the protagonist.
Character	Role	Personality
Elder Rowan	Village elder and keeper of local legends	Wise, theatrical, slightly forgetful
Bram	Retired lumberjack in Riverwood	Gruff, practical, fond of tall tales
Mira	Mountaineer near the northern gate	Adventurous, observant, dry humour
